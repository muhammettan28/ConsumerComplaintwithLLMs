\title[Local LLMs for Financial Complaint Classification]{Local Consumer Complaint Classification: Zero-Shot, Lightweight Adaptation and Label Budgets}

\author[1]{\fnm{Muhammet} \sur{Tan}}\email{muhammettan28@gmail.com}
\author*[1]{\fnm{Halit} \sur{Bak{\i}r}}\email{halit.bakir@sivas.edu.tr}

\affil*[1]{\orgdiv{Department of Computer Engineering}, \orgname{Sivas University of Science and Technology}, \orgaddress{\city{Sivas}, \postcode{58010}, \country{Turkey}}}

\abstract{Local classification of consumer financial complaints must balance
accuracy, label availability, inference latency and GPU memory. We study
these engineering constraints on a five-category Consumer Financial
Protection Bureau task using an archived zero-shot benchmark of eleven
open-weight models, supervised classifiers and controlled lightweight adaptation.
The preserved protocol comprises balanced and reduced-imbalance conditions,
three test subsets and paired 250/500-character narratives. We adapt
Gemma~4~12B, Qwen3~14B and Phi-4~14B with QLoRA using 500 balanced
training records and 2,000 separate validation records. Each adapter is compared
with its own base model under the same Hugging Face revision, NF4
quantisation, chat template and thinking-disabled configuration. On the
pooled 3,000 balanced 500-character records, weighted F1 increases from
0.7520 to 0.7803, 0.7099 to 0.7891 and 0.6883 to 0.7845, respectively.
Paired bootstrap intervals are positive in all twelve model--condition--length
comparisons, and no adapted prediction is outside the taxonomy.
Training takes approximately 35--41 minutes with 7.77--10.04~GiB peak
allocated memory on a 16~GB RTX~5060~Ti. Warm serial mean latency ranges
from 0.435 to 0.647~s for adapted models; adaptation does not uniformly
reduce latency. TF-IDF/SVM reaches F1~0.7546 with 500 training records
and 0.8291 with 20,000, while three DistilBERT fits average 0.8244 at
20,000. These unequal label budgets support conditional deployment
choices rather than a universal model ranking. Historical API comparisons
remain contextual, and the single adaptation training seed limits
generalisability.}

\keywords{consumer complaint classification, large language models, QLoRA, label budgets, CFPB, local deployment}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau (CFPB) publishes complaint
narratives that can support regulatory oversight and financial-service
routing. Misclassification may delay a complaint or send it to the
wrong department, motivating both accuracy and operational reliability
\cite{das2024explainable,liu2024framework,jondhale2024cfpb,di2023complaint}.
Banks and regulators may also need to keep new complaint text on local
infrastructure rather than transmit it to third-party APIs.

Supervised text classifiers require task-specific training labels;
zero-shot instruction-following models instead receive the taxonomy
in a prompt \cite{devlin2019bert,brown2020gpt3,kojima2022zeroshotcot}.
This reduces adaptation effort when taxonomies change, but introduces
engineering costs in generation latency, output parsing, and hardware.
A model that invents an unsupported label cannot be routed safely through
a system that assumes a fixed category set.

The closest published comparison, Roumeliotis~et~al.\
\cite{roumeliotis2025think}, evaluates 14 commercial model APIs on the
five-category CFPB task. Its best result is o1 at accuracy 0.777 and
weighted F1 0.7764, with a reported historical API charge of
\$24.24 per thousand predictions. We examine whether local open-weight
models provide competitive zero-shot performance, and how that choice
changes when labelled training data are available. We evaluate balanced
and reduced-imbalance samples, paired narrative-length variants,
invalid outputs, and inference latency. Exact commercial test identifiers
are unavailable, so cross-study comparisons remain protocol-matched
rather than instance-identical.

We additionally ask whether 500 labelled complaints suffice to improve
three representative LLMs within a 16~GB GPU budget, and whether those
gains justify their training and serving costs relative to direct
classifiers. The supervised experiments share a separate 2,000-record
validation set; training-label budgets exclude these additional labels.
QLoRA enables this test through low-rank updates to frozen
quantised weights \cite{hu2021lora,dettmers2023qlora}. The experiment
preserves every existing test file and selects adapters using validation
loss, keeping test outcomes outside model selection.

Our contributions are:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item We benchmark eleven open-source, open-weight LLMs —
        \texttt{gemma2:9b}, \texttt{gemma4:12b}, \texttt{mistral:7b},
        \texttt{mistral-nemo:12b}, \texttt{llama3.1:8b}, \texttt{phi4:14b},
        \texttt{deepseek-r1:8b}, \texttt{deepseek-r1:14b},
        \texttt{qwen3:8b}, \texttt{qwen3:14b}, and \texttt{gpt-oss:20b}
        — on the same five-category CFPB zero-shot task, at
        \textbf{zero API cost}. This expanded model set includes matched
        8B/14B variants for Qwen3 and DeepSeek-R1, enabling within-line
        scale comparisons; other family comparisons also change model generation.

  \item We measure lightweight adaptation of Gemma~4~12B, Qwen3~14B
        and Phi-4~14B using 500 balanced labels, with paired adapters-on/off
        inference at identical model revisions and runtime settings,
        serial latency, training time and peak allocated GPU memory.

  \item We conduct a second experiment using a reduced-imbalance
        class distribution, evaluating whether performance patterns
        observed under artificial balance hold in a more realistic
        scenario.

  \item We provide a direct quantitative comparison with the paid-API
        results of Roumeliotis~et~al.\ \cite{roumeliotis2025think},
        showing that our best open-weight model has a numerically higher
        score than GPT-4o under a matched evaluation protocol and remains
        competitive with more expensive API-based alternatives.

  \item We analyse off-label prediction behaviour — instances where
        models invent labels outside the five prescribed categories —
        as a proxy for instruction-following reliability.
  \item We add supervised classical and DistilBERT baselines using
        training/validation data separated from every saved test subset,
        together with paired bootstrap uncertainty, multiplicity-adjusted
        accuracy tests and equal-prior reweighting. The classical learning
        curve separates 500/2,000/10,000/20,000-label budgets; LLM adaptation
        is evaluated at 500 labels and one training seed.
\end{enumerate}

The remainder of this paper is structured as follows.
Section~\ref{sec:related} surveys the related literature.
Section~\ref{sec:data} describes the dataset and preprocessing
pipeline. Section~\ref{sec:setup} details the experimental setup.
Section~\ref{sec:results} presents results for both experiments.
Section~\ref{sec:discussion} discusses the findings.
Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes with directions for future work.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

Classical complaint classifiers use engineered text features and
supervised learners. Word embeddings and contextual transformers
extend these representations \cite{lilleberg2015word2vec,devlin2019bert}.
Prior CFPB work applies topic models and transformer classifiers,
while complaint prioritisation and explainability studies address
operational deployment \cite{raju2022bertopic,grootendorst2020bertopic,
vairetti2024bert,das2024explainable,liu2024framework}.

Instruction-following LLMs enable zero-shot classification alongside
summarisation and response generation \cite{brown2020gpt3,zhao2023survey,
correa2024genai}. Related tourism, e-commerce, and customer-service
studies compare generative and supervised models, but their taxonomies
and data splits differ from this task \cite{roumeliotis2024tourism,
roumeliotis2024ecommerce,li2024customerservice}. Recent analyses of
LLM-assisted complaint writing provide additional motivation for
robustness studies on newer corpora \cite{shin2025llmwritten,liang2025cfpb};
our 2020--2021 data do not directly test that shift.

Reasoning-oriented models generate intermediate text before an answer
\cite{wei2022chainofthought,deepseekllm2024,guo2025deepseekr1,qwen2025qwen3}.
Roumeliotis~et~al.\ \cite{roumeliotis2025think} provide our closest
five-category zero-shot reference. Bulgakov and Turchin
\cite{bulgakov2026deepseek} instead study binary monetary-relief
prediction with a distilled local model. G{\"u}ll{\"u}~et~al.\
\cite{gullu2025llama} report 89.99\,\% accuracy for supervised
LLaMA~3.1~8B; their different split and training procedure make this
context rather than a target or directly comparable ceiling.

Low-rank adaptation freezes base parameters and learns small task-specific
updates \cite{hu2021lora}; QLoRA combines these updates with NF4,
double quantisation and paged optimisation \cite{dettmers2023qlora}.
We use these established methods to test the practical value of limited
complaint labels on consumer hardware.
Our contribution is an empirical local deployment comparison across
label budgets and output-reliability requirements, with preserved test
sets for supervised baselines. We do not claim a new LLM architecture.
The extended background discussion is supplied in the supplementary material.

\section{Dataset and Preprocessing}
\label{sec:data}

\subsection{Data Source}

We use the CFPB Consumer Complaints dataset originally published by
Tiwari \cite{tiwari2021cfpb} on Kaggle, which is a processed subset
of the public CFPB Consumer Complaint Database. The dataset covers
complaints submitted between March~2020 and March~2021 and contains
three columns: an unnamed index, \texttt{product} (the category label),
and \texttt{narrative} (the free-text complaint). The raw file
comprises 162,421 records consolidated to five target product categories:
\texttt{retail\_banking}, \texttt{credit\_reporting},
\texttt{credit\_card}, \texttt{mortgages\_and\_loans}, and
\texttt{debt\_collection}. This is the same dataset and label set used
by Roumeliotis~et~al.\ \cite{roumeliotis2025think}, ensuring
direct comparability.

\subsection{Class Distribution}

The original data are heavily imbalanced. After removing records with
empty \texttt{product} or \texttt{narrative} fields (10 rows removed,
162,411 remaining) and discarding narratives exceeding 500 characters
(resulting in 103,915 usable records), the class distribution shifts
to: \texttt{credit\_reporting}~63.6\,\%,
\texttt{debt\_collection}~14.1\,\%,
\texttt{mortgages\_and\_loans}~7.7\,\%,
\texttt{credit\_card}~7.5\,\%, and
\texttt{retail\_banking}~7.1\,\%. Before the length filter, the same
five-category corpus contains 162,411 records with
\texttt{credit\_reporting} accounting for 56.1\,\% of the data.
Fig.~\ref{fig:class_dist} illustrates how this skew propagates into
the experimental subsets: the imbalanced condition retains the
dominant \texttt{credit\_reporting} class at 51.1\,\%, while the
balanced condition enforces equal class sizes of 200~samples each.
At $N{=}1{,}000$, a model that defaults to the majority class in the
imbalanced setting would achieve 51.1\,\% accuracy with zero
language understanding — motivating the need to report both conditions
side by side.

\subsection{Preprocessing Pipeline}

The preprocessing steps, implemented in Python using pandas, are applied
in the following order to every record:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item Drop rows with empty \texttt{product} or \texttt{narrative}.
  \item Retain only records belonging to the five target categories.
  \item Remove narratives longer than 500 characters, reducing processing
        time and GPU memory pressure while avoiding truncation artifacts
        during inference.
  \item Remove exact duplicate narratives before normalisation.
  \item Standardise narrative text: convert to lowercase, remove special
        characters, and normalise whitespace.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Experimental Subsets}

Two experimental conditions are defined, each evaluated under a
multi-seed, multi-size design to ensure statistically reliable estimates.

\textbf{Experiment~A (Balanced):} 200~records are sampled uniformly at
random from each of the five categories (N=1,000 total) using
stratified undersampling.
This follows the evaluation protocol of
Roumeliotis~et~al.\ \cite{roumeliotis2025think}; because the original
sample identifiers are not publicly released, the comparison should be
interpreted as protocol-matched rather than instance-identical.

\textbf{Experiment~B (Reduced imbalance):} The saved subsets have a
less skewed distribution than the post-filter corpus, with at least
100~samples per class. They do not sample the unaltered natural
distribution. Each contains $N{=}1{,}000$ with
\texttt{credit\_reporting}~51.1\,\%,
\texttt{debt\_collection}~18.2\,\%,
\texttt{mortgages\_and\_loans}~10.7\,\%,
\texttt{credit\_card}~10.0\,\%, and
\texttt{retail\_banking}~10.0\,\%.
The dominant \texttt{credit\_reporting} class means a naive majority
baseline would reach only 51.1\,\% accuracy with no language understanding.
This second experiment, absent from the closest antecedent benchmark,
assesses how class imbalance affects both accuracy estimates and
per-class performance patterns.

\textbf{Multi-seed and narrative-length design:}
Each condition is replicated with three subset identifiers (1, 2,
and 3) and at two narrative character-length limits (250 and 500
characters), yielding 12 datasets per model and 132 inference runs in
total (11~models $\times$~3~seeds~$\times$~2~conditions~$\times$~2~length
limits). The 250-character subsets contain exactly the same records in
the same order as their 500-character counterparts, with narratives
truncated to 250 characters. Balanced and reduced-imbalance subsets
share records and are therefore not independent replications.
The archived Ollama result tables use the 500-character variant and
mean~$\pm$~standard deviation across the three test subsets. New HF
adaptation comparisons instead pool 3,000 predictions per mode and
condition, with paired cluster-bootstrap intervals. The three test
identifiers are distinct from the single LLM adaptation training seed.
The 250-character runs provide a narrative-length
sensitivity check discussed in Section~\ref{sec:sensitivity}.
This design addresses the single-seed limitation common in prior
zero-shot LLM benchmarks and allows us to distinguish robust ranking
patterns from seed-specific artefacts.

The two length variants compare truncated and retained text from the
same short-narrative population. The narrative-length histogram is
provided in Supplementary Fig.~S1.

\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\subsection{Infrastructure}

The new supervised and paired HF adaptation experiments run on an
Ubuntu workstation with an Intel Core i7-14700 processor, 32\,GB RAM,
and an NVIDIA GeForce RTX~5060~Ti with 16\,GB VRAM. The adaptation
environment uses Python~3.12, PyTorch~2.14.1, Transformers~5.18.0,
PEFT~0.21.2 and bitsandbytes~0.50.2. Model revisions, dataset and adapter
SHA-256 hashes, package versions and run statuses are saved. The HF
evaluation uses cached weights in offline mode; complaint narratives
are not sent to a third-party model service.

The archived zero-shot benchmark uses Ollama, a self-contained serving runtime
that manages model loading, KV-cache management, and the local REST API.
Its Python inference script communicates through a local REST endpoint.
Historical artifact digests, thinking modes and execution logs are
incomplete; the saved results do not establish every historical hardware
or serving configuration. New HF timing is consequently reported
separately from archived Ollama timing.

\subsection{Models}

Table~\ref{tab:models} lists all eleven models. The original grouping
treated five builds as reasoning models and six as standard models.
However, upstream Gemma~4 supports configurable thinking \cite{google2026gemma4card};
the exact thinking mode of the saved \texttt{gemma4:12b} runs was not
recorded and its grouping remains unverified.
Only Qwen3 and DeepSeek-R1 provide within-line 8B/14B pairs;
other comparisons mix parameter count, generation, and training recipe.
The builds use compressed GGUF weights. Current local metadata
identifies \texttt{qwen3:8b} as Q4\_K\_M and
\texttt{gpt-oss:20b} as MXFP4, so a uniform Q4\_K\_M claim is
inappropriate. Current metadata does not establish the identity of
every historical artifact; missing digests remain a reproducibility limitation.
The largest model, \texttt{gpt-oss:20b}, requires 13.0\,GB VRAM and
fits within the 16\,GB local GPU memory budget.
The evaluated model families build on recent open-weight model lines,
including LLaMA \cite{touvron2023llama2}, Mistral \cite{jiang2023mistral},
Gemma \cite{team2024gemma2}, Phi \cite{abdin2024phi4}, Qwen
\cite{qwen2025qwen3}, and DeepSeek \cite{deepseekllm2024,guo2025deepseekr1}.
Model names in Table~\ref{tab:models} are the exact Ollama tags used
in the experiments. The family column records the upstream lineage or
registry metadata associated with each tag and should not be interpreted
as equivalence to a proprietary API model with a similar provider name.
For this archived zero-shot benchmark, artifacts were obtained through
\texttt{ollama pull <tag>} without task-specific fine-tuning. The new
adaptation experiment uses separately pinned HF weights, as detailed below.
\texttt{gpt-oss:20b} is an open-weight reasoning model released by
OpenAI and distributed through the Ollama registry; it requires no API
key and its weights are publicly accessible via
\texttt{ollama pull gpt-oss:20b}.
The ``OpenAI'' entry in the Family column reflects the model's
originating developer, not access through any proprietary API or
commercial service.

\subsection{Prompt Design}

A single, model-agnostic prompt is used for all models and all
experiments. The prompt follows the five-label JSON-output template
used by Roumeliotis~et~al.\ \cite{roumeliotis2025think} to ensure
comparability:

\begin{quote}
\textit{You are an AI assistant specializing in consumer complaint
classification. Your task is to analyze a consumer complaint and
classify it into the most appropriate category from the predefined
list: ["retail\_banking", "credit\_reporting", "credit\_card",
"mortgages\_and\_loans", "debt\_collection"]. Provide your final
classification in the following JSON format without explanations:
\texttt{\{"product": "chosen\_category\_name"\}}. Complaint: [NARRATIVE]}
\end{quote}

For the archived Ollama runs, temperature is set to 0 to reduce
sampling variability and improve reproducibility. No explicit
\texttt{top\_p}, \texttt{max\_tokens}, stop-sequence, or model-specific
thinking-mode flags are set; Ollama defaults are used for all other
decoding parameters. Although the prompt asks for JSON without
explanations, some reasoning-oriented builds may still emit
intermediate text before the final answer. The inference script
therefore extracts the final JSON object from the response by searching
for the last valid \texttt{\{"product": "..."\}} occurrence using a
regular expression, accounting for models that prepend reasoning traces
before the answer.

The inference script was implemented using only Python standard-library
modules (\texttt{csv}, \texttt{json}, \texttt{urllib}, \texttt{time},
\texttt{argparse}), eliminating the need for any \texttt{pip}-installed
dependencies on the compute node. Each request is retried once on
HTTP failure before being recorded as an error; errors are included in
the final count. Saved responses are limited to 2,000 characters and
newlines are replaced with spaces. The legacy parser also falls back
to a category mentioned anywhere in the response when no JSON match
is found, which can accept explanatory text as a classification.
The CSVs omit separate thinking text, termination reasons, and token
counts, limiting post-hoc auditing.
The full experiment was orchestrated as local batch runs totalling
132 tasks (11~models~$\times$~12~datasets), with runs scheduled
sequentially or in controlled parallel groups according to GPU memory availability.

\subsection{Evaluation Metrics}

Following the closest antecedent benchmark, we compute four primary metrics over
all $N{=}1{,}000$ predictions per run: \emph{exact-match accuracy},
\emph{weighted precision}, \emph{weighted recall}, and
\emph{weighted F1-score}. We additionally report \emph{macro-F1},
which weights all classes equally and is especially informative for
the imbalanced experiment. Weighted variants weight each class by its
sample size, making them appropriate for comparison with the reference
paper but potentially optimistic under class imbalance. We additionally
record the \emph{mean
latency} (seconds per prediction, wall-clock time) and the number
of \emph{off-label} predictions — responses where the model returned
a category name not in the prescribed five, or failed to return
valid JSON entirely. Off-label predictions are counted as errors.
The API cost for all experiments is \$0. Compute time is supplied by
the local workstation rather than a commercial LLM API.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Experiment A: Balanced Evaluation}

Table~\ref{tab:results_balanced} presents the full results for
Experiment~A, reported as means over three test subsets.
The \texttt{gemma4:12b} model achieves the
highest accuracy ($0.774 \pm 0.017$) and weighted F1 ($0.774 \pm 0.016$),
numerically exceeding the larger
\texttt{gpt-oss:20b} (F1~$0.749 \pm 0.019$) and ranking first among
all eleven saved model runs. Its unrecorded thinking mode prevents
using this result as evidence that reasoning is unnecessary.
Second place goes to \texttt{qwen3:14b} (F1~$0.750 \pm 0.011$),
a reasoning model, followed by \texttt{gpt-oss:20b} in third.
\texttt{deepseek-r1:14b} (F1~$0.724 \pm 0.007$) and
\texttt{qwen3:8b} (F1~$0.717 \pm 0.002$) complete the top five.
The standard \texttt{phi4:14b} (F1~$0.706 \pm 0.011$) ranks sixth.
Notably, \texttt{deepseek-r1:8b} (rank~7, F1~$0.683 \pm 0.012$)
only narrowly exceeds the standard \texttt{gemma2:9b} (rank~8,
F1~$0.676 \pm 0.009$), confirming that 8B reasoning models do not
automatically dominate similarly sized standard models. The weakest model is \texttt{mistral-nemo:12b}
(F1~$0.540 \pm 0.025$, rank~11), which despite being a 12B model
scores below both \texttt{mistral:7b} (F1~$0.587$) and
\texttt{llama3.1:8b} (F1~$0.587$), suggesting that the Mistral Nemo
architecture is not well-suited to strict JSON output tasks in the zero-shot
setting. The low standard deviations across most models —
notably \texttt{qwen3:8b} ($\pm$0.002) and \texttt{mistral:7b}
($\pm$0.004) — show limited score variation across these test subsets;
small standard deviations alone do not establish every rank separation.

the top-five (\texttt{qwen3:14b}, \texttt{gpt-oss:20b}, and
\texttt{qwen3:8b}) are all reasoning models. The two models
with severe format-following failures are standard models:
\texttt{llama3.1:8b} produces 67 off-label predictions and
\texttt{mistral:7b} produces 59. Importantly, \texttt{gemma4:12b}
(rank~1) produces only 3.7 invalid predictions on average.
Its saved failures are empty responses recorded as parse errors,
rather than valid out-of-taxonomy labels. Their cause cannot be
determined from the retained response field alone. At the same time, \texttt{phi4:14b}
is notable among standard models: it reaches F1~0.706 with only one
off-label prediction and a mean latency of 0.54~s, making it the
strongest low-latency alternative.

We perform post-hoc exact McNemar tests (binomial, one-sided) on
paired correctness outcomes for each of the three seeds independently,
applying a Bonferroni-adjusted threshold of $\alpha{=}0.0125$ (four
comparisons anchored on \texttt{gemma4:12b}).
The advantage of \texttt{gemma4:12b} over \texttt{deepseek-r1:14b} and
\texttt{qwen3:8b} is significant in all three seeds ($p{<}0.001$ each),
providing robust statistical evidence for those rank separations.
Comparisons with \texttt{qwen3:14b} and \texttt{gpt-oss:20b} reach
significance in only one seed each. Non-significance in other subsets
does not establish equality, and McNemar tests accuracy rather than F1. The two lowest-performing
models (\texttt{llama3.1:8b}, \texttt{mistral-nemo:12b}) are clearly
separated from all others by a wide margin.

For Experiment~A, macro-F1 is numerically identical to weighted F1
because all five classes have equal support. Per-class inspection
reveals that even the best model is uneven across labels:
\texttt{gemma4:12b} reaches F1 0.825 on \texttt{retail\_banking} and
0.820 on \texttt{mortgages\_and\_loans} but drops to 0.718 on
\texttt{debt\_collection} and 0.743 on \texttt{credit\_reporting}.
This motivates the macro-F1 reporting in Experiment~B, where
minority-class behaviour can otherwise be hidden by the majority class.
Detailed per-class F1 for the leading models are reported in
Table~\ref{tab:per_class}.

Supplementary Figs.~S2--S5 show the balanced F1 distribution and
confusion matrices for gpt-oss, Phi-4, and DeepSeek-R1~14B.
Mortgage/loan and credit-card confusions remain visible even for
models with almost no invalid outputs. Llama~3.1's larger invalid-output
count primarily reflects refusals rather than invented category names.

higher accuracy and F1 compared to Experiment~A.

with mean accuracy $0.782 \pm 0.017$ and F1 $0.783 \pm 0.016$.
\texttt{gemma4:12b} follows closely in second place
(Acc~$0.776 \pm 0.015$, F1~$0.779 \pm 0.014$), and

The top five models (\texttt{gpt-oss:20b}, \texttt{gemma4:12b},

be interpreted as genuine robustness improvements, because both models

\subsection{Narrative Length Sensitivity}
\label{sec:sensitivity}

Supplementary Fig.~S8 compares mean accuracy on
250-character-limit versus 500-character-limit narratives for the
balanced condition (both at $N{=}1{,}000$). The overall ranking
is highly stable: no model changes its relative rank by more than
one position between the two length limits. Most models improve by
0--3 percentage points moving from shorter to longer narratives, with
\texttt{deepseek-r1:14b} (+1.6~pp) and \texttt{llama3.1:8b} (+2.8~pp)
showing gains when additional text from the same complaints is retained.
Pairing all 3,000 balanced predictions and applying Holm correction
over 22 model--condition tests gives significant accuracy changes for
\texttt{llama3.1:8b} ($p_{\mathrm{adj}}<0.001$) and
\texttt{qwen3:14b} ($p_{\mathrm{adj}}=0.0234$).
The DeepSeek-R1~14B change does not pass this corrected threshold
($p_{\mathrm{adj}}=0.0879$).
\texttt{gpt-oss:20b} and \texttt{mistral-nemo:12b} show virtually no
change (approximately 0.13~pp and 0~pp, respectively), indicating little observed sensitivity
to narrative length in this range. The stability of the top-cluster
ranking suggests that the ordering is not driven solely by these two
length settings; this does not establish robustness to longer narratives.



\subsection{Latency Analysis}

Mean per-prediction latency is shown in the Lat.(s) column of
Tables~\ref{tab:results_balanced} and~\ref{tab:results_imbalanced}.
Standard models are notably faster: \texttt{mistral:7b} completes
a prediction in 0.23~s on average, while \texttt{phi4:14b} requires
only 0.54~s despite being twice the parameter count. Reasoning models
are slower: \texttt{deepseek-r1:14b} takes 9.33~s per prediction due
to its end-to-end generation behaviour. \texttt{gemma4:12b}
has the highest latency at 17.1~s. Separate thinking output, token
counts, and termination reasons were not saved, so this latency
cannot be attributed to a specific generation mechanism. The
latency–accuracy trade-off is model-type dependent: \texttt{phi4:14b}
achieves the best latency-accuracy ratio among all models (0.54~s,
F1~0.706). The full Experiment~B F1 plot is supplied as
Supplementary Fig.~S7.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Reasoning vs.\ Standard Models}

\texttt{gemma4:12b} achieves the highest weighted F1 in
Experiment~A ($0.774 \pm 0.016$), numerically exceeding
\texttt{gpt-oss:20b} (F1~$0.749$). However, Gemma~4 supports thinking,
and the saved runs did not record whether it was active. This benchmark
therefore compares deployed model configurations rather than isolating
the benefit of reasoning. A controlled thinking-on/off comparison on
identical complaints is needed to support that causal claim.

At the 8B tier, \texttt{deepseek-r1:8b} and \texttt{qwen3:8b} generally
outperform similarly sized \texttt{gemma2:9b}, \texttt{llama3.1:8b},
and \texttt{mistral:7b}. At 14B, \texttt{qwen3:14b} (F1~0.750) and
\texttt{phi4:14b} (F1~0.706) have different point estimates. These
comparisons also change architecture and training recipe and cannot
isolate a reasoning effect \cite{wei2022chainofthought,guo2025deepseekr1}.

An unexpected finding is the underperformance of \texttt{mistral-nemo:12b}
(F1~$0.540 \pm 0.025$ in Experiment~A, rank~11 of~11), which falls
below both its smaller sibling \texttt{mistral:7b} (F1~$0.587$) and
all other 12B-tier models. Its off-label rate is low (0.7 per run),
indicating that the model does produce parseable JSON but assigns
incorrect categories. This suggests that the Mistral Nemo architecture,
while competitive in general-purpose tasks, does not transfer as
effectively to this narrow structured classification setting.

\subsection{Within-Family Performance Patterns}

Within the Qwen3 line, 14B exceeds 8B by 0.033 F1 points; the
corresponding DeepSeek-R1 gain is 0.041. Both are within-line scale
comparisons. Gemma~4 exceeds Gemma~2 by 0.098, but changes generation,
size, and potentially thinking mode. Mistral-Nemo~12B falls below
Mistral~7B despite having more parameters. These observations establish
performance differences rather than their architectural causes.
Detailed comparisons are supplied in the supplementary material.

\subsection{Output Reliability and Inference Latency}

Invalid predictions count as errors in accuracy, recall, and F1.
Llama~3.1~8B records a mean of 67 parse errors in Experiment~A and
57 in B, often explicit refusals. Mistral~7B records 59 and 42
out-of-taxonomy labels, usually parseable JSON. Both failure modes
remain substantial. They require different remediation: response
format enforcement does not by itself prevent refusals or resolve
ambiguous labels. Reasoning-enabled builds generally show fewer
invalid outputs, but architecture and alignment also differ, preventing
a causal attribution to reasoning.

Among saved zero-shot runs, Phi-4~14B combines weighted F1~0.706 with
0.54~s mean latency; Mistral~7B is faster at 0.23~s but much less
accurate. DeepSeek-R1~14B takes 9.33~s. Recorded latency reflects
end-to-end server requests, rather than pure decoding throughput;
separate thinking, token counts, and termination reasons were not
retained. The supervised baselines use direct classifier inference
and their new serial latency measurements include feature/token preparation.

\subsection{Deployment Recommendations}

Deployment choices depend on available labels and the serving runtime.
With 500 training labels and the common validation set, all three HF
adapters improve their own matched base models, while TF-IDF/SVM
provides a lower-latency option with pooled balanced F1~0.7546.
At 20,000 training labels, SVM and DistilBERT have higher point
estimates than the 500-label adapters and much lower measured serial
latency. This is a comparison of deployment regimes, not equal-budget
evidence that either model family is universally superior.

For local LLM adaptation, Phi-4 has the largest balanced F1 gain and
the lowest adapted serial mean latency (0.435~s), while Qwen3 has the
highest balanced adapted point estimate (0.7891). Their small F1 difference
was not tested as a between-model superiority claim. Unmerged adapters
increase measured serial latency for Gemma and Qwen; merging, alternative
serving engines and concurrent request throughput were not evaluated.
The following archived Ollama recommendations apply to their saved
zero-shot configurations; they are not timing predictions for HF NF4.

\textbf{Maximum accuracy (offline / batch):}
Use \texttt{gemma4:12b}. It achieves the highest balanced F1 (0.774),
is numerically close to published paid-API scores, and has a reported
artifact size of 7.6~GB. At 17.1~s per prediction, one million serial
predictions require approximately 198~GPU-days on the measured
workstation. Large archive processing would require independently
measured batching or multiple GPUs; their speedup was not evaluated.

\textbf{Real-time deployment ($<$1\,s latency required):}
Use \texttt{phi4:14b}. It offers F1~0.706 at just 0.54~s per
prediction — the best accuracy-to-latency ratio among all eleven models.
At roughly 110~predictions per minute throughput, it can serve a
moderate complaint routing queue in real time on the local GPU.

\textbf{Smaller model artifacts:}
Consider \texttt{qwen3:8b} (5.2~GB artifact, F1~0.717, 5.19~s);
peak runtime VRAM must be checked separately. It produces zero
off-label predictions and outperforms all other sub-6\,GB models.

\textbf{Avoid} \texttt{mistral:7b} and \texttt{llama3.1:8b} in
zero-shot settings: both produce $>$50 off-label predictions per
1,000 samples on average, making them operationally unreliable
without post-processing.
\texttt{mistral-nemo:12b} is also not recommended for this task
despite its size advantage.

Taken together, these recommendations translate into a concrete cost
advantage. At moderate scale (one million complaints per year),
deploying \texttt{gemma4:12b} instead of the best-performing paid API
model (o1) would avoid approximately \$24,240 in API fees at the
historical reference rate. This is an API-fee comparison, not a total
cost-of-ownership estimate or proof of equal quality. \texttt{qwen3:14b} and \texttt{gpt-oss:20b} similarly
have point estimates above the published GPT-4o baseline (F1~0.7358)
without API fees. This
should not be read as computation being free: local inference still
consumes GPU time, electricity, and local hardware resources. The
practical gain is the elimination of recurring third-party API charges
and the need to transfer sensitive complaint text to external services.

Equal-prior reweighting in Section~\ref{sec:audit} shows that most of
the observed Experiment~B gains reflect class proportions. Model
quality should be assessed with macro-F1 and class-specific metrics;
partly shared test records preclude treating A and B as independent
replications.

\subsection{Limitations}

This study has several limitations. First, the balanced comparison
matches the reference protocol but not necessarily the exact sampled
instances, since the original sample identifiers are unavailable.
Second, the 500-character length filter reduces inference cost but
also shifts the class distribution, especially increasing the share of
\texttt{credit\_reporting}; future work should test whether the same
patterns hold for longer narratives. Third, lowercasing and special-
character removal may discard useful cues such as account-number
patterns, capitalization, dates, or punctuation; the effect of less
aggressive LLM-oriented preprocessing remains untested. Fourth, all experiments use $N{=}1{,}000$ examples per seed,
evaluated at two narrative character-length limits (250 and 500
characters) across a five-class taxonomy; larger sample sizes,
longer narratives, and richer taxonomies may reveal additional
drift and rarer category confusions.
Fifth, all local models are evaluated with a single zero-shot prompt.
Prompt wording, output-format instructions, few-shot examples, or
self-consistency decoding may change the relative ranking of models,
particularly for smaller standard models with weaker instruction
following. Sixth, the zero-shot models use compressed Ollama builds,
including differing quantisation formats, so scores conflate model family, model
size, and quantisation effects. Seventh, we cannot rule out pre-training
contamination: open-weight models may have encountered CFPB complaints
or derivative datasets during pre-training, although no task-specific
fine-tuning is used in the LLM zero-shot runs. Eighth, LLM adaptation uses
only 500 training records and one training seed. Its three test subsets
and bootstrap intervals do not measure variation across independent
adapter fits; larger training budgets and additional training seeds remain
unevaluated for LLMs. The common validation set adds 2,000 labelled records
to each reported supervised training budget. Serial latency is measured on
100 deterministic, class-balanced examples per mode after warmup, excluding
response decoding and network serving overhead. Batch and serial outputs
differ on three of 600 benchmark predictions; the full-test F1 estimates
describe the saved batched outputs. Ninth, the reported \$0 cost
refers to API expenditure, not to the full economic cost of GPU
hardware, electricity, or local workstation operation. Finally, the main tables
report weighted aggregate metrics; for operational deployment,
macro-F1 and per-class precision/recall should be inspected to avoid
masking poor minority-class performance.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We compared archived zero-shot LLM configurations, controlled QLoRA
adaptation and direct supervised classifiers on preserved CFPB tests.
Using 500 balanced training labels and a common 2,000-label validation
set, Gemma~4, Qwen3 and Phi-4 improve pooled balanced weighted F1 by
2.83, 7.92 and 9.62 percentage points under matched HF inference.
Improvements remain positive in all twelve model--condition--length
comparisons, with no invalid adapted output. Training fits a 16~GB GPU
and takes approximately 35--41 minutes. Adapted serial mean latency
remains below 0.65~s in the measured benchmark, but adaptation does not
uniformly accelerate inference.

The 20,000-training-label SVM and DistilBERT baselines achieve higher
F1 point estimates with much lower classifier latency, so available
labels and serving constraints determine which local approach is useful.
Weighted scores under reduced imbalance must be interpreted alongside
macro-F1 and class-specific results. The historical Ollama/API comparison
provides zero-shot context, without establishing statistical equivalence
or isolating reasoning effects. Future work should repeat LLM adaptation
across training seeds and larger label budgets, evaluate longer narratives
and temporal shifts, and measure alternative serving configurations.
